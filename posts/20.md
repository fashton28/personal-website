Over the years, I’ve learned some pretty cool shit.
I’ve gotten to experience great things that have led me to find purpose and live a fulfilling life.
And heck, I’m only turning 20; there’s still so much to live for out there, and so much to look forward to.
But after two decades, I think I’ve gotten pretty good at distilling some principles I try to live by.
So here are 20 lessons I’ll try to refine as the month passes.
I’ll be 19 for one more month, which may still give me some time to add even more insight.

Each day, I think more and more about what it means to turn 20.
It means ending a decade of greatness: of being a teenager, of fleeting youth.
I am a present human being, yet I have to be even more present and ambitious than ever.

Here are some of the lessons I learned in my teens, and my north star for this great decade that’s just starting.

1. Be a good human being.
   It’s as simple as being empathetic, patient, and, most importantly, optimistic.
   Being a good human implies having the certainty that humanity’s future is pretty damn bright and that you have the agency to shape it.

   That leads me to the next point.

2. Be someone with high agency.
   Act upon your ideas.
   Don’t let them sink; take charge of them and become the captain of those thoughts.
   Shape them and do something good for the world with them.

3. Create obsessively.
   In a world of overconsumption, creating should be the default if you want to be excellent.

4. Master focus.
   All great feats of humanity have been achieved by individuals who have mastered focus.
   It comes with practice, but it becomes easier with a system.
   This is pretty high level, but fundamentally, my protocol has been simplified to the following:

    1. Work insanely hard (and smart).
       Plan, but not excessively; plan just enough to keep some structure.
    2. Work in long blocks.
       I enjoy working in blocks of 4 to 16 hours.
       Now that I’m in college, it’s really hard to extend my sessions beyond 4 hours, but I try to do so as much as I can.
    3. Think for yourself.
       Flow can only be achieved when you’re in a state of creation, when your brain is working aggressively and thinking systematically about hard problems.
       It’s hard to focus on easy things, so strive to work on hard, challenging tasks.
    4. Do work that feels like play.
       I hate busy work.
       Sometimes it must be done, but I truly hate it.
       Having a meeting at some point in my day is even worse.
    5. Work as hard as you can.
       I try to align myself with work that I truly love, and then I work as hard as I can.
       I can work hard because I enjoy it.
       It’s hard to explain, but enjoying the work is what lets me keep going for extended periods of time.
        1. Work at blistering intensity.
    6. Train like you’ve never won.
       Compete like you’ve never lost.
        1. If your work is heavily intellectual, treat yourself like an Olympic athlete.
           Sleep, food, and recovery are all fundamental.
           It’s exceptionally important that you take care of all of these things.
        2. The mentality of an elite athlete also translates here.
           Play the game like one.
           Work like you’ve never won, and compete in business, technology, programming, or whatever you love like you’ve never lost.
           Have that confidence and you’ll go far.
        3. Do what sucks first.
           Even though I enjoy most things, there’s always something I want to do less than everything else.
           I tend to get those things out of the way first.
           I don’t always want to work out or run, but I still do it first thing every morning because I know my willpower will diminish as the day goes on.

5. Find the work that feels like play to you.
   To me, that’s programming, thinking about business, building things that help people, going down rabbit holes that make me more technical, running, and working out.

6. Don’t become complacent with your current life.
   You can always go a step further.
   Look at the greats in history.
   They never became complacent once they became emperors or millionaires.
   They believed in a mission and always went a step further.
   Go the step further.
    1. Take more risks.
       Learn to enjoy the feeling of uncertainty.
       Take the harder class, educate yourself, and do what you thought you couldn’t.
       I’ll have a story to tell by the end of the semester…

7. Health is exceptionally important.
   Don’t fall into the trap of putting it aside.
   Most people aren’t in great shape by 30.
   Aim not to be average.
   Without health, you can’t work or care for others.
   It’s truly the foundation of a great life.

8. Aim to be ahead of the curve.
   If you think about what average looks like, it’s a terrible spot to be in.
   For that reason:
    1. Aim to become elite at what you do.
       No matter the industry, being in the top 0.01% will be exceptionally rewarding in every sense of the word.

9. Have high integrity.
   Do not cut corners, but that doesn’t mean you shouldn’t strive to find ways to make things faster or more efficient.

10. Read a lot.
    Read about a vast array of topics, from finance to biology.
    During my late teens, I started leaning more toward reading about technology and biographies of great people.
    1. Don’t read self-improvement books; read biographies.
       There’s no better way to find guidance on improving yourself than by going through entire lifetimes of lessons.
       These books aren’t lessons learned after a decade.
       They are made up of lessons learned over a lifetime.
    2. Use them as a way to find archetypes you align with.
       Perhaps your way of being resonates with that of Steve Jobs or Walt Disney.
       You can learn so much just from being aware of the similarities between you and some of the greatest minds that have ever existed.

11. Don’t be afraid of change.
    My teens probably came with more change than the average person experiences in a decade.
    I changed cities, schools, countries, friendships, knowledge, and opportunities.
    I tried a vast array of things that helped me discover my passions.
    Change is good.
    You should embrace it and learn to adapt rapidly.

12. Be constantly grateful for your life.
    Be grateful for the simple things: the cup of coffee in the morning, and the fact that you can wake up every day and just do things.

13. Live with no regrets.
    You can’t change the past, so always be forward-looking.
    This doesn’t mean you can’t learn from past mistakes; that’s precisely why you shouldn’t regret doing things.
    You will learn from them massively.
    1. Of course, don’t do things that might kill you or put you in jail.
       That’s why I tend to avoid parties, drinking, and any activity that increases the chances of something like that happening.

14. Go for the challenging things.
    People are scared of taking a class because it will be “hard.”
    Sure, that may be true, but the grueling experience will help you grow as a person.

    Over time, you’ll develop a sort of mental callus, where what’s exceptionally difficult for others will be easy for you.

    This applies not only to intellectual domains but to everything in life.
    Run the marathon, take the class, start the company.
    Failing is never an option.
    Either you win or you learn.

    During my teenage years, I tried doing plenty of hard things: moving to a new country, taking the hardest courses I could, and attempting the impossible until it became possible.

15. Find intellectual mentors.
    Have people in history you look up to.
    Even better, find someone you see yourself in.
    Strive to be like them, avoid their mistakes, and move forward that way.
    You don’t have to be a 1:1 copy.
    The greatest people in history were inspired by other great people.
    Stand on the shoulders of giants.

16. Be an honest human being.
    Do things with exceptional honesty.
    This is the best way to live.

17. Time freedom is the best freedom.
    Finding the work that feels like play helps with this.
    It’s even better if that work is something you can do from wherever you want.
    Having location and time freedom is the best freedom!
    Whether you choose to build in SF or around the world is really up to you.

18. Be at the frontier of technology.
    Being young gives you an immense advantage and the energy to keep up with the frenetic pace of technology.
    Take advantage of it, whether you are an engineer or not.
    Be at the frontier.

19. Time is your greatest asset.
    Don’t waste it.

20. Two decades.
    Time flies, and here I am, enjoying every bit of it.
    There is no other way to live.
